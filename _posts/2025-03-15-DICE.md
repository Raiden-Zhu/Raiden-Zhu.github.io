## Main Results

**Theorem (Approximation of r-hop DICE-GT)**

The r-hop DICE-GT influence $$ \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{DICE-GT}}^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{z}\_j^t, \boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}) $$ can be approximated as follows:

$$
\begin{equation}
\begin{split}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{DICE-E}}^{(r)}(\boldsymbol{z}_j^t, \boldsymbol{z}^{\prime})
= - &\sum_{\rho=0}^{r} \sum_{ (k_1, \dots, k_{\rho}) \in P_j^{(\rho)} } \eta^{t} q_{k_\rho} 
\underbrace{ \left( \prod_{s=1}^{\rho} \boldsymbol{W}_{k_s, k_{s-1}}^{t+s-1} \right) }_{\text{communication graph-related term}} \\
& \times
\underbrace{ \nabla L\bigl(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k_{\rho}}^{t+\rho}; \boldsymbol{z}^{\prime}\bigr)^\top }_{\text{test gradient}} \\
& \times
\underbrace{ \left( \prod_{s=2}^{\rho} \left( \boldsymbol{I} - \eta^{t+s-1} \boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k_s}^{t+s-1}; \boldsymbol{z}_{k_s}^{t+s-1}) \right) \right) }_{\text{curvature-related term}} \\
& \times
\underbrace{ \Delta_j(\boldsymbol{\theta}_j^t,\boldsymbol{z}_j^t) }_{\text{optimization-related term}}
\end{split}
\end{equation}
$$

where $$ \Delta\_j(\boldsymbol{\theta}\_j^t,\boldsymbol{z}\_j^t) = \mathcal{O}\_j(\boldsymbol{\theta}\_j^t,\boldsymbol{z}\_j^t)-\boldsymbol{\theta}\_j^t $$, $$ k\_0 = j $$. Here $$ P\_j^{(\rho)} $$ denotes the set of all sequences $$ (k\_1, \dots, k\_{\rho}) $$ such that $$ k\_s \in \mathcal{N}_{\mathrm{out}}^{(1)}(k\_{s-1}) $$ for $$ s=1,\dots,\rho $$ and $$ \boldsymbol{H}(\boldsymbol{\theta}_{k_s}^{t+s}; \boldsymbol{z}_{k_s}^{t+s}) $$ is the Hessian matrix of $$L$$ with respect to $$ \boldsymbol{\theta} $$ evaluated at $$ \boldsymbol{\theta}_{k\_s}^{t+s} $$ and data $$ \boldsymbol{z}_{k\_s}^{t+s} $$.

For the cases when $$ \rho = 0 $$ and $$ \rho = 1 $$, the relevant product expressions are defined as identity matrices, thereby ensuring that the r-hop DICE-E remains well-defined.

## Background

Training frontier AI models is becoming astronomically expensive, with costs running into the hundreds of millions of dollars.[1] This trend risks centralizing AI power within a handful of tech giants, locking out academia and smaller players. **Decentralized Learning** offers a compelling escape route, where data and compute are crowdsourced across a peer-to-peer (P2P) network.[2] Think of it like a computational barn-raising, where a community comes together to build something no single member could alone.

But this collaborative utopia hits a snag: **incentives**. Why would anyone contribute their data and compute without a fair way to measure and reward their effort? This is the core problem we tackle. We need a reliable "Proof-of-Work" for collaborative learning, and that starts with accurately valuing each contribution.

## Why Old Tools Don't Work

In the centralized world, data valuation is often handled by methods like Leave-one-out (LOO) or the famous Influence Functions from Koh and Liang.[1] These tools work by asking a simple question: "How does the model's performance change if we remove this one data point?"

But these methods stumble in a decentralized setting. There's no single, global model to measure against; every participant has their own. More importantly, influence isn't a static, one-off calculation. It's a dynamic process. An update on one node *flows* to its neighbors during training, who then pass it on to *their* neighbors. It's a cascade.

This forced us to rethink what "value" even means here. We found an answer in a surprising place: the customer-centric philosophy of management guru Peter Drucker.[1] He argued that the value of a product is determined by what the *customer* gets out of it. In our world, when a node shares its model update, its neighbors are the customers. The value of that contribution, therefore, should be judged by the nodes that receive it.

## DICE: Following the Data's Ripple Effect

With this new perspective, we built DICE to formally model the influence cascade. We developed a ground-truth definition of influence that accounts for its spread across multiple hops in the network, and then derived a practical, gradient-based method to estimate it.

Our key theoretical result, **Theorem 2**, gives a precise mathematical characterization of this multi-hop influence cascade.

## The Physics of Influence

The formula looks intimidating, but it describes a beautiful physical process. We can break it down into four intuitive parts that govern how influence is born, travels, gets shaped, and is finally measured.

| Component | Role | Intuition |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Optimization Term** | The Initial Push | This is the initial "nudge" a data point gives to its own local model. It's the stone dropping into the water, creating the first disturbance. |
| **Communication Term** | The Propagation Path | This term tracks the path the influence takes through the network, determined by the communication weights between nodes. It defines how the ripple spreads. |
| **Curvature Term** | The Influence Modulator | This is the most subtle part. Intermediate nodes aren't just passive relays; their own learning state (the curvature of their loss landscape) actively shapes, dampens, or amplifies the influence passing through them. They are like lenses focusing or scattering the signal. |
| **Test Gradient Term** | The Measurement Probe | This measures the final effect. After the influence has propagated, how much did it actually change the model's performance on a test point at the destination? This is how we see the ripple's impact on the other side of the pond. |

## Key Insights from the Theory

This framework doesn't just give us a formula; it reveals the underlying dynamics of decentralized systems:

1.  **Topology is Power:** Your position in the network graph dictates your influence. Nodes that are better connected act as amplifiers, spreading their data's impact far and wide.[1]
2.  **The Network is an Active Medium:** The path influence takes is not inert. The learning state of every intermediate node actively modulates the signal, meaning a data point's value is dynamic and depends on the state of the entire network at that moment.[1]
3.  **Influence Fades:** Just like a real ripple, influence naturally decays as it travels across the network. The combined effects of communication and curvature create a "damped decay".[1]

## From Theory to Practice

The full multi-hop theory is powerful, but for everyday use, we can distill it into a lightweight and efficient tool we call **Proximal Influence**. This simply measures the direct, one-hop influence a neighbor's data has on your model. Because it only uses information that is already being shared during standard decentralized training (like gradients), it's incredibly efficient to compute.[1]

This simple tool unlocks some powerful applications:

  * **Detecting Bad Actors:** Malicious data (e.g., with flipped labels) or "free-riding" nodes that don't contribute meaningfully can be easily spotted. Their proximal influence scores will be negative or near-zero, making them stand out clearly. This allows participants to dynamically prune low-quality neighbors and secure the network.[1, 3, 4]

## The Big Picture: Building Fairer AI

DICE is more than just a data valuation method; it's a toolkit for **decentralized governance**. It gives network participants the tools to ensure **security** (by spotting bad actors) and **fairness** (by measuring reciprocity).

This work lays the foundation for robust incentive mechanisms, a critical step toward unlocking the massive potential of decentralized learning. Looking ahead, we believe DICE provides a dynamic, contribution-based pricing model that could power the next generation of **decentralized AI ecosystems**, including data markets, parameter markets, and compute markets, fostering a more open and equitable AI future.[1]
